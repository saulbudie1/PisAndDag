# PART I — GROUND RULES

*What everything else has to obey. Read this part before writing a line of anything.*

---

## §I. The Nature of the World

The world is not governed by a simple struggle between good and evil.

It is governed by **principles**.

The gods are embodiments of forces that existed before mortal civilization: Life, Birth, Death, Sky, Order, and perhaps others not yet understood. None of them are benevolent in the ordinary sense. Each takes the truth it embodies and carries it beyond its natural boundary until that truth becomes monstrous.

The central metaphysical tension is:

> **A principle becomes monstrous when it refuses to acknowledge its opposite.**

Life refuses Death.  
Birth refuses consequence.  
Death has forgotten how to end.  
Order refuses defiance.  
Shimmer refuses permanence.  
The ancient infectious forces impose their own absolutes upon reality.

Mortals, meanwhile, repeatedly attempt to understand, exploit, contain, or kill the things that govern them.

---

## §II. Central Thematic Structure

The current mythology can be summarized through opposing absolutes:

| Force | Healthy Principle | Monstrous Extreme |
|---|---|---|
| **Life** | Survival | Nothing may die |
| **Birth** | Creation | Create without consequence |
| **Death / Mors** | Ending | Death has forgotten how to end |
| **Loi** | Order | Absolute authority |
| **Shimmer** | Possibility | Nothing has a fixed identity |
| **Corruption** | Decay | Everything must end immediately |
| **Crimson** | Life | Everything must become one |
| **Hallow** | Purity | Everything must be perfect |
| **Moon Lord** | Unknown | Reality rewritten into something alien |
| **The Low Court** | Accountability | Justice without restraint |
| **Mechanic** | Innovation | Creation without ethical limits |

The world is therefore not a war between **Good and Evil**.

It is a world in which fundamental truths have been pushed beyond their proper boundaries.

The player slowly discovers that the ancient catastrophes are interconnected:

**The gods distorted their domains.**

**Mortals tried to control the gods.**

**Civilizations built machines and seals to contain forces they barely understood.**

**The Archmage broke Death to free humanity.**

**The Low Court set out to learn how to kill gods, and has not managed it.**

**The Seal was broken by the player.**

And beneath all of it lies the oldest question:

> **If the gods themselves cannot understand when enough is enough, why should mortals trust them to rule the world?**

---

## §III. The Spine

**[LOCKED — the through-line, for keeping new material honest]**

> **The player is always someone else's instrument.**

The learned houses wrote a key into a bloodline so that someone, one day, could open the lock (§XXVII). The demons carry the effigy so the player will burn it. The Low Court put the orb where the player would find it (§XVII, §XXX). The Mechanic hands over work she will not finish (§XXI).

Nobody in this world asks the player to do anything. They arrange for it to be the obvious next step.

The Pilgrim is the counterweight and the only one (§XXXI). Death's gift at the end is the last use anyone gets out of the player, and it is meant kindly (§XXXII.3).

New material should be tested against this: **who benefits, and does the player know?**

---

## §IV. Register

**[LOCKED]**

Soulsborne throughout — Dark Souls 1–3 and Elden Ring rather than Greek myth or high fantasy.

What that means in practice:

- **The catastrophe already happened.** The player arrives late to every story in this book. Nothing is prevented; things are only answered for.
- **Nobody out-fights a god.** A god is killed by finding the rule that makes it unkillable and breaking that instead (§XVIII).
- **The successful ones never fight.** Patience and arrangement beat strength every time, and the calmest person in a faction is the most dangerous.
- **Orders are found in pieces.** Scripture split and hidden apart, apparatus rusting where it was abandoned, a signature carved on a statue centuries before the player learns whose it is.
- **Consequences land on people who did not consent.** That is the cost of every exception anyone has ever made to the rules of this world.
- **Gods are approached, not summoned** (§IX).
- **Questlines can be missed and the game does not say so** — after the restoration (§XIV).
- **Nothing the player fights is in its prime.** See below.

### Nothing in Its Prime

**[LOCKED]**

Every god, Lord and order the player meets is a late version of itself. Mors has lost his memory. Birth spent herself making Fishron. Life has grown past regulation because nothing prunes. The Kingdom of Grace is rubble, the Low Court inherited a library and lost it, and the Archmage is three hundred years old and cannot rest.

Two exceptions.

The **Moon Lord** is the only power in the world still at full strength, which is why gods who are not are afraid of him.

The **Mechanic** is the only mortal at hers, which is why she is the one who dies.

## §V. Lore-Writing Direction

The desired storytelling style is **mythological archaeology** rather than exposition dumps.

The player should rarely receive the complete truth directly.

Lore should emerge through:

- boss introductions
- item descriptions
- NPC dialogue
- ruins
- architecture
- inscriptions
- contradictory historical accounts
- environmental storytelling
- weapons with forgotten owners
- ancient paintings
- damaged books
- enemy behavior
- recurring symbols
- obscure questlines
- dialogue that becomes meaningful only much later

The player should frequently encounter something whose meaning is unclear, then discover its significance many hours later.

A seemingly insignificant line such as:

> *“No one remembers his name.”*

can eventually become the revelation that **the world itself has forgotten Death.**

The mythology should feel ancient, fragmented, contradictory, and larger than the player's immediate understanding.

The gods should rarely explain themselves.

**Let the ruins accuse them.**

# PART II — THE GODS

*Six forces, and the things mistaken for them. Each one is a truth carried past its own boundary.*

---

## §VI. The God of Life

The God of Life embodies **continuation, growth, survival, regeneration, adaptation, and biological existence**.

She is not a goddess who lovingly protects every living thing. Her concern is more primal and terrible:

> **Life must continue.**

Individual suffering, identity, dignity, and even natural limits are secondary to continuation.

She can therefore manifest life in grotesque forms:

- organisms that refuse to die
- impossible regeneration
- uncontrolled adaptation
- biological fusion
- resurrection
- endless growth
- creatures that continue living long after life should have ceased

Life regards Death not as an enemy worthy of hatred, but as an intolerable interruption.

### Life and Mors

**[LOCKED]**

They were the working pair. She grew it, he ended it, and for an age that arrangement was the whole mechanism of the world. Neither resented the other. Ending is what makes room, and Life has always needed room.

So Life is the god who lost most when he stopped. Nothing prunes. Growth with no edge to push against runs away from whoever is holding it, and she has been holding it for three hundred years. The Jungle is not only the poison the Low Court put into it (§XVII). It is a forest that nothing finishes.

Her quarrel is with Birth, and it is old. Birth begins things and walks away from them; Life will not let anything she has been handed stop. Between the two of them the world fills up with creatures neither will answer for.

### The Dryad

**[PROPOSAL / DEVELOPING]**

The Dryad may be a vessel for the God of Life, carrying a fragment or expression of divine vitality.

The Dryads may have historically served as vessels for portions of Life's essence, making them more than mortal beings even if they do not understand the source of their nature.

This would explain the unusual relationship between Dryads, the natural world, and the ancient conflicts surrounding the world.

### Plantera

**[PROPOSAL / DEVELOPING]**

Plantera may be an ancient or failed vessel of Life.

Rather than taking a humanoid form, an immense concentration of Life's essence became a colossal organism whose instincts are almost entirely:

> **Grow. Adapt. Survive.**

Plantera is not necessarily evil. She is life stripped of moderation.

The Jungle may therefore contain some of the oldest living matter in the world, with Plantera acting as an ancient expression of Life's refusal to perish.

---

## §VII. The God of Birth

The God of Birth is separate from the God of Life.

Birth embodies **creation, emergence, conception, generation, possibility, and beginnings**.

Where Life demands that something continue existing, Birth demands that **something new come into existence**.

Her flaw is not simply lust. It is an insatiable compulsion toward creation.

She is capable of forming intimate unions and may possess a mythological reputation for seduction and promiscuity, echoing ancient fertility and patriarchal myths without reducing her character to sexuality.

Her true pathology is:

> **She cannot stop creating, yet refuses to accept responsibility for what she creates.**

She may create beings out of fascination, desire, curiosity, boredom, or obsession. When a creation disappoints her, she may abandon it or destroy it rather than accept its existence as something independent of her will.

### The Stolen Dagger

Birth once stole one of Mors's life-ending daggers.

The dagger was never designed to cause death. It was a ceremonial instrument used by Mors to complete an ending that had already become inevitable.

Birth discovered another use for it:

> **She could create without having to live with every consequence of creation.**

A failed creation could simply be ended.

A creature that displeased her could be discarded.

A new attempt could begin.

This became part of the horror of Birth: **creation without accountability**.

The same instrument represents opposite philosophies:

**Mors:**  
> The end has already come. Do not make it linger.

**Birth:**  
> This one failed. Make another.

### The Abominations

**[LOCKED]**

What Birth discards, Life refuses to let end, and Death can no longer finish.

That recipe dates them: every abomination in the world is post-amnesia. Before the Archmage, a failed creation was discarded and it stayed discarded — the dagger worked, and Mors closed what she opened. Now her rejects are caught between a mother who has walked off and a goddess who will not permit an ending, and they keep going.

They are not demons and not experiments. They are children nobody will finish.

### Duke Fishron

Birth used herself as an incubator to create Duke Fishron.

The process consumed and transformed her.

Fishron became one of her greatest and most disturbing creations. He inherited something of her compulsive hunger: the drive to consume, create, and continue the cycle.

Yet Fishron may eventually come to despise the existence forced upon him.

This creates a tragic inversion:

> **Birth cannot stop creating.**  
> **Fishron cannot stop existing.**  
> **Mors cannot properly make anything end.**

Fishron's desire for an ending should be treated as existential tragedy rather than romanticized self-destruction.

---

## §VIII. Mors — God of Death

Mors is not merely a god associated with death.

> **Mors is Death given consciousness.**

He once remembered every death.

Every mortal.  
Every beast.  
Every battlefield.  
Every quiet death in a forgotten room.

He knew when a life had reached its final moment and performed the necessary function of ending it.

Death was not cruel. It was simply **final**.

### Death Cannot Die

**[LOCKED]**

This is the one hard rule of the world.

Other gods can be felled. Death cannot. Ending is what he *is*, and a thing cannot be ended by the function it performs.

This is why the Archmage's crime takes the shape it does. He did not fail to kill Death — killing Death was never available to anyone. He did the only thing that was: he wounded what cannot be ended and left it running.

That is worse than murder, and it produces the book's central irony:

> **Death's opposite is continuation. Mors now continues, endlessly, unable to make anything stop.**

He has become the thing he is the opposite of — the exact failure §I describes, suffered by the one god who did not choose it.

### The Archmage's Crime

The Archmage discovered that Mors's memory was intimately bound to the finality of death.

The Archmage believed permanent death was an injustice.

He therefore attempted the impossible:

> **He erased Death's memory.**

He did not destroy Mors's body.

He mutilated his identity.

Mors forgot:

- who he was
- what his purpose was
- the deaths he had witnessed
- who was dead
- who was alive
- even that he himself was Death

The consequences were catastrophic.

#### Respawning

Because Death no longer properly recognizes completed deaths, mortals can return.

The world's inhabitants have effectively inherited a form of immortality through **Death's amnesia**.

Respawning is therefore not a blessing granted by a benevolent force.

It is the symptom of a cosmic injury.

The Archmage genuinely believed he had saved humanity.

## §IX. Loi — God of the Sky

**Lôi**, God of the Sky, may also embody **sovereignty, authority, natural order, and dominion from above**.

He believes that everything beneath the heavens should remain within the order of the sky.

He is not benevolent.

His order can become authoritarian.

He despises:

- gods who interfere with his domain
- mortals who defy divine hierarchy
- the Low Court
- forces that overwrite natural order

His philosophy is essentially:

> **Nothing beneath the heavens has the right to defy the heavens.**

#### Vessel / True Form

**[PROPOSAL]**

Loi may initially appear through a vessel or avatar.

The vessel believes itself to be Loi, but may actually be only a fragment or extension of the greater entity.

The deeper revelation could be that Loi does not truly possess a body.

> **The sky itself is his domain — perhaps even his body.**

#### Reaching Him

**[LOCKED]**

Loi is not summoned and does not come down. **The player goes up, and has to be noticed first.**

Notice is earned by transgression, in three steps, none of them signposted:

1. **The shout.** Breaking the orb (§XXX) asks the world Shimmer's question out loud. That is the loudest heresy available against a god whose law is that nothing beneath the heavens may defy the heavens. He hears it, and the world tightens in answer.
2. **The trespass.** Flight after the Empress of Light (§XXIX) is entering the heavens without permission. The first two steps are things the player does for other reasons and only later learns were counted.
3. **The third** is whatever is done up there — undecided.

The player is not granted an audience. They accumulate a record.

#### Armageddon

**[LOCKED — the animal, the owner, the name, and that it is worse. Where the fight sits is
PROPOSAL.]**

Loi keeps a wyvern.

It is old, it is three times the size of the thing in the Font (§XIX), and it is worse
than her. She fought because it was her shift. This one has an appetite.

**It is a pet.** Not a guardian and not a weapon and not a punishment sent down. It is
kept, and fed, and exercised, and named, and the name is Armageddon.

The name is not something the world gave it out of fear. It is what its owner calls it. A
god who is not benevolent, and whose order can become authoritarian, named his animal
after the end of everything, and then took it out.

The wyverns the player has been killing in the sky since the first island are his as well.
Nothing in the game says so. It becomes available the moment somebody says whose the big
one is, and the player does the rest without help.

**[PROPOSAL] It is the third step.** *Reaching Him* leaves the third transgression
undecided, and notes that the shout and the trespass are things the player does for other
reasons and only later learns were counted. Killing a god's animal is the third — and the
only one of the three the player knows is a transgression while they are doing it.

##### What It Carries

**[LOCKED]**

**The fire.** Stone goes to lava. Water is not there any more. Dirt is ash. It does not
damage the ground, it converts it, and every surface the player was standing on is a
different substance afterwards. There is no arena at the end of this fight. There is what
the fire left.

**The lightning.** It arrives before the roar. The chest fills first and the air tears
open along it, and then the sound comes. That is the only warning in the fight and it is a
generous one. A thing this size can afford to be fair about the worst of what it does.

**The claw.** One of them, not both. What it leaves is a disease and what the disease does
is grow: trees come out of the bone. An elephant has two hours. A man has less. There is a
cure and the clock starts when the claw lands.

**[PROPOSAL] The claw is on the blind side.** He swings that hand where he cannot see, so
it does not come where it is aimed — it comes where he last knew somebody was. The safest
way past a one-eyed animal is the side it cannot watch, and that is the side with the
disease on it. The whole fight is that sentence.

##### Where the Three Come From

**[LOCKED — the split. The third is OPEN and meant to stay that way.]**

The fire is his. It is what the animal is.

**The lightning is not his. It is the sky's.** He carries his owner's weather, and that is
the plainest statement in the book of what a pet of Loi is: the thing that comes down when
something beneath the heavens has stepped outside the order. The player has been fighting
smaller versions of him under that same weather since the first floating island.

**[OPEN] The claw is neither.** Nothing about the sky grows trees out of anybody, and
nothing about the animal does either. A pet carrying something that belongs to neither
itself nor its owner is a question, and the book asks it by putting the three side by side
and saying nothing. It sits with the one already open below — made, found, or inherited.

##### The Catch

**[LOCKED]**

He is injured when the player meets him.

One eye is gone. The hide around the wings is pierced through.

Neither is fresh. The player never sees what he was before this and never will. The thing
in the sky is the reduced one, and it is still worse than anything else they have fought.

*(Note for whoever builds both: the other flying thing in this book also ends with holes
through her wings, and one of them falls and one of them is still up. Nothing points at
it.)*

##### When

**[LOCKED]**

**After Mors remembers.** This fight sits in the *After* (§XIV): death works, it sticks,
and it takes whatever was unfinished with it.

Which is the whole reason to put it there. The first great fight in a world that can end
things is against an animal named for the end of everything, and the man who comes with
the player is the one who has spent the entire game unable to finish anything. Nothing in
the encounter says any of that.

It also confirms what §XIV already implies and the book should stop hedging about: the
*After* is playable content, not a credits roll.

##### The Flaps

**[LOCKED]**

The wings move the player. Not knockback — displacement. The Ankh Shield does not hold,
and nothing else does either.

That is not a difficulty knob. It is the god's sentence rendered as a mechanic: *nothing
beneath the heavens has the right to defy the heavens*, and the Ankh Shield is the
player's standing claim that they can hold their ground against anything. This is the one
thing in the game that reads that claim and moves them anyway.

Build note: knockback immunity is a real flag and this must not go through it. Apply the
motion directly, make the wing-beat legible before it lands, and never take the player's
controls away — they keep every input, they simply end up somewhere else.

##### Who Did It

**[LOCKED — the two wounds, and that they have different authors. The occasion and the
reveal are PROPOSAL.]**

Two wounds, and they are not the same kind of wound, and that is the first thing anybody
looking at him should notice.

**The wings were an army.** Hide punched through in a pattern, from below, many times. No
single thing does that. It was the goblin military, all of it, the same engineering that
out-built a kingdom (§XIX, §XXI) turned upward. Nobody in particular is responsible. That
is the point of an army.

**The eye was one person.** An eye has to be taken at arm's length, by somebody who got
that close and had a reason to. **It was Pugream's teacher.**

**[PROPOSAL] The occasion.** Loi despises mortals who defy divine hierarchy, and the
goblin empire was beating a kingdom that claimed the favour of heaven. So the sky sent its
animal down on the goblins, on the side of the claim — and the goblins shot it. It is the
book's thesis at full volume: steel over blessing, workmanship over legitimacy, and this
time not against a lord with a claim but against the thing the claim was about. Grace lost
anyway.

##### Pugream

**[LOCKED — that he was there and did nothing.]**

He was there. He was the student.

**He did not fight it and he did not hurt it.** He watched his teacher get close enough to
take an eye, and he lived, and his teacher did not come back from it. That is the entire
content of his connection to this animal: he is the one who got away, and he has been
walking ever since.

He has never mentioned it.

It is why he comes to this fight and to no other, why he knows the name, and why he will
not say how. And it turns every frame of him the player has already watched: the getting
up that takes more frames than the falling is not a brave animation, it is what he has
instead of what his teacher had.

**[PROPOSAL] How the player finds out: the animal does it.** From the moment the Pilgrim
is in the room it turns its head to keep him on the good side. Not because he hurt it —
because he was standing behind the one who did, and it remembers. It goes for him first.
He lets it look at him. Nobody says anything, and the player watches an animal recognise a
man who did nothing.

##### He Does Not Die Here

**[LOCKED]**

Death works now (§XIV). He could. He does not.

**If the player goes down, he is flapped away.** The same wing-beat that no shield in the
game resists is what takes him out of it, alive, and that is not a mercy the animal
decided on. It moved him the way it moves everything. It has never once considered him
worth killing.

He is the one it did not bother with, then and now. The book does not say this out loud
anywhere and does not have to; the player watches it happen.

Build note: he is never killed on screen and there is no state in which the player has
failed him. He leaves the way weather leaves things.

##### What He Spends

**[LOCKED]**

He fights it with a great bow that works, and the bow breaks on it.

That is what he has and that is what he does with it. No speech, no oath, no last stand —
a man puts everything he owns into one animal, it is not enough, the wind takes him off
the field, and afterwards he hands the player the pieces (§XXXI, *The Greatbow*).

The player has seen the weapon at full draw, once, in somebody else's hands.

**[GUARDRAIL — the two ways this gets ruined]**

**Not the avenger.** The obvious shape for a marked man returning to a maimed dragon is
the screaming avenger, and a Souls-literate player will arrive braced for exactly that. He
has no speech at the gate, no cry at the kill, no oath, no lifetime of hate. He is calm,
and the calm is not stoicism, it is a debt he has never been able to pay. It only reads if
nothing in the scene announces it. He says nothing about the eye, before, during or after;
if he speaks at all it is about something else, and every line the fight wants to give him
goes to the animal's head turning instead.

**Not the heir.** His teacher took an eye, so the pull will be to make him the inheritor of
whatever did it — a technique, a weapon, a bloodline. There is none. §XXXI is not
negotiable: not chosen, not secretly the strongest man alive, significance from
persistence rather than power. He learned nothing that helps. He is not much use in this
fight and the fight must let that be true.

**[OPEN — and the timeline has already half-answered it]** Nothing stayed dead (§XIV), so
his teacher never died of it; somebody who got that close to a god's animal and did not
get to finish has been out there the whole time. But this fight is in the *After*. The
restoration has already landed, and it took whatever was unfinished with it.

So the thing that finally killed Pugream's teacher is the thing the player did.

Not answered here: whether he knows, whether he was there for that one too, and whether
the walk to this fight started before the restoration or the day after it.

**[OPEN]** Whether Pugream has ever gone looking.

**[OPEN]** Whether Loi made it, found it, or inherited it. Whether it was always malicious
or became so in his keeping.

## §X. The Moon Lord

**[DEVELOPING / PROPOSAL]**

The Moon Lord may not be a conventional god at all.

Mortals call him the Moon Lord because they lack a language capable of describing what he actually is.

Unlike the other gods, he may have no recognizable conceptual domain.

He is something **outside the existing divine order**.

The Celestial Pillars may represent his attempt to reshape reality, making the world compatible with his existence.

His alien nature is precisely what frightens the other gods.

## §XI. Shimmer

Shimmer is not another god.

It is a primordial substance or phenomenon existing **between states of reality**.

It does not merely alter appearance.

It alters **what something is**.

Its underlying principle is:

> **What if you were something else?**

This makes Shimmer fundamentally uncomfortable to the gods.

Life says:

> Grow.

Birth says:

> Begin.

Death says:

> End.

Loi says:

> Remain within order.

Shimmer says:

> **You could become something else entirely.**

It may therefore be older than the current divine hierarchy.

### The Lock

**[LOCKED]**

Shimmer is the antidote to what was done to Mors, and the Archmage understood that before anyone else did.

His crime was committed against an identity. Shimmer is the one force in the world that changes what a thing *is* — the only power that could give Death back the fact of being Death. So he sealed it, and sealed it early, while the world was still working out what had gone wrong.

What the player finds in pools and seams is seepage from a bad lock. It works on small things, unreliably. The rest of it is somewhere else, and it is his second crime: the first one made the wound and this one made it permanent.

#### What He Locked

**[LOCKED]**

He did not hide it. He could not have; it is a substance and there is a great deal of it.

He locked what it **does**.

Shimmer is still in the ground in the quantities it always was. What is gone is the
question it asks. The pools the player finds are not a fugitive god in hiding — they are
the same liquid as before with nothing behind it, and it is transportable, storable,
ladlable, ordinary. A man can carry a jug of it across a room. Before, a man carrying a
jug of it across a room would have been taking a risk with what he was.

That is the shape of the second crime. Not theft. **Disarmament.** He took the one thing
in the world that could have given Death back the fact of being Death and left it lying
about everywhere as water.

What still works on small things is the lock leaking, and it is worth being precise about
which way it leaks: not enough to change a person, enough to change an object. The player
will spend a whole game using Shimmer for exactly the sort of trivial transmutation that
proves the great thing is switched off, and will not notice.

**[OPEN]** Whether the lock can be opened, and whether the Archmage still holds the key
or put it somewhere he could not reach either.

## §XII. The Lords

Lords are distinct from gods. A **Lord** is a being who has attained extraordinary sovereignty, influence, or dominion without necessarily embodying a fundamental force of reality.

A Lord may be mortal, altered, divine-blooded, or something stranger. Some rule civilizations; others are remembered as Lords because their power, deeds, or authority became too great to describe them as ordinary mortals. The title has no single universal requirement and may be historical, cultural, or self-proclaimed.

Some Lords were worshipped as gods despite never truly being gods. Others ruled without worship at all.

> **Gods embody principles. Lords possess dominion.**

A Lord can fall. A Lord can be killed. A Lord can be forgotten.

The existence of Lords keeps the world's mythology from becoming a simple hierarchy in which every powerful figure is automatically divine. Mortal history has its own legends, conquerors, rulers, saints, monsters, and failures.

#### Avatars and Manifestations

A manifestation of a god is not necessarily the god itself. A divine force may produce an **Avatar**, vessel, offspring, echo, or other physical expression without the resulting being possessing the full nature of the deity.

An Avatar may therefore be fought and killed without the underlying god ceasing to exist.

**Life Avatar** is one possible example: a physical manifestation of the God of Life rather than Life itself. Its existence should reveal something about Life without simply replacing the goddess as a boss.

# PART III — THE WOUND

*One mortal broke the rule that made endings work. Everything here is downstream of that.*

---

## §XIII. The Archmage

The Archmage is the mortal responsible for the mutilation of Death.

He believed he had accomplished something miraculous:

> **He freed humanity from permanent death.**

He acted out of conviction. On his own premises he was right, and nobody in this book has argued him down.

### What He Learned from the Houses

**[LOCKED]**

He was not one of the learned houses. He was the scholar they brought in (§XXVII, The Quiet Work), and he left that project carrying the method rather than the doctrine.

Two things he learned building the Seal became his crime. The first is that nothing unkillable is out-fought — you find the rule that makes it unkillable and break that instead (§XVIII). The second he watched them do: the houses wrote the key into a bloodline in front of him, and so he learned that a person can be a container, and that what is kept in one can be reached.

From there, memory as the place where finality lives is one step. His crime is the Seal's own method turned around, and the houses taught it to him.

### He Did Not Die

**[LOCKED]**

He is still alive, three hundred years on, living on his own injury. He is the only person in the world who remembers it working.

He is frail in the way everything else in this book is frail (§IV). He is not hiding and he is not raising anything. He cannot stop, and he has had a very long time to think about that.

He does not want the wound closed. Restoring Mors kills him and concedes that he was wrong, and he is still not certain that he was. That is the whole of his resistance, and it is enough: the player's central act is the one thing in the world that ends him.

### [PROPOSAL] The Archmage Boss

His fight could violate ordinary rules of reality:

- attacks that rewind
- duplicated selves
- delayed wounds
- temporary disappearance of his hitbox
- self-revival
- manipulation of the player's death/respawn behavior
- attempts to reproduce his original experiment

A possible final-phase philosophy:

> **If Death can forget once, it can forget again.**

Defeating him does not automatically restore Mors.

The wound to Death is older than the fight.

---

## §XIV. The Two Eras of Death

**[LOCKED — this is lore first and a game rule second]**

The world has two states, and the boundary between them is Mors getting his memory back (§XXXII.3).

### Before

Nothing stays dead, and that includes the town.

The Merchant comes back from a goblin arrow for exactly the same reason the player comes back from a fall: Death forgot him too. This is not a concession to the way Terraria works. It is the condition the Archmage created, applied honestly to everyone.

So for most of the game, **death costs nothing and breaks nothing.** No questline is lost to a blood moon. No exception is made for a player who kills someone on purpose — one rule is worth more than a clever one, and the clever one would be indistinguishable from a bug.

### After

Death works. It sticks, for everyone except the player (§XXXII.3).

From that point a death is permanent and **takes whatever was unfinished with it.** The world has already told the player the rules changed: the town stopped fighting, the Nurse got serious, somebody said one line about it. That is fair warning in the way this book prefers to give it — the world said so, nothing put it in a tooltip.

Note what this rules out: the town does **not** become invulnerable. The whole content of the restoration is that things can end now, and a town that suddenly cannot die would say the opposite at the exact moment it matters. NPCs can be killed and they stay killed. The lore-required deaths are not a special case — they are simply the first ones.

### How Long

**[LOCKED]**

Three hundred years, give or take, from the Archmage's working to the player's arrival.

The length is doing work. Nobody alive remembers death functioning; permanent ending is a rumour, a heresy, and in some tellings a slander against the man who saved everybody. The Low Court had time to inherit a library and lose it. The Kingdom of Grace was already ruins when the Court convened. And nobody in town can be shocked by the amnesty, because none of them has known anything else. They can only be shocked by its ending.

### What It Looks Like

**[LOCKED]**

The amnesty has to be visible or it is a rule in a document. Three things carry it, and none of them is explained.

**The un-rotted.** Old battlefields where the dead are still lying where they fell, intact, because decay is an ending and endings do not land. Nothing has gone to bone. Nothing has gone to anything.

**The recut stone.** Graves in the old yards carrying one name and four death-dates, each scratched out and carved again beneath the last. Somebody kept paying the mason.

**The overstayed.** Things in those battlefields that come back together once after they are killed, and fight on. After the restoration they die properly, which is the only announcement the change ever gets.

### Who Wants It Back

**[LOCKED]**

The restoration has opposition, and the opposition is sympathetic. Everyone alive is living on the amnesty and most of them are glad of it.

**Against.** The Party Girl, cheerfully, which is the worst of them. The Tax Collector, who came back from Hell and keeps accounts for people who would rather he did not stop (§XVII). Anybody in the world with a child back.

**For.** The Princess, raised inside a claim of divine favour that was never true, who wants a finite life on purpose (§XX). Truffle, whose whole ecology requires endings (§XXVIII). The Nurse, who has seen what comes back. The Heretic, who worked it out alone and has not got the nerve to say so (§XVII).

The player is never polled. The town simply starts saying different things once it knows what is being carried.

### Two Who Live On It

**[LOCKED]**

Two mortals have built a life out of the wound. Both are found underground. Neither is a boss.

**The Clerk.** He decided that if Death cannot remember, a man will. He has been recording every death in the world for three hundred years — in books, properly, with dates — and he calls himself the Second Memory. Everybody calls him the Clerk, which annoys him. He is a pedant. He will correct the player's phrasing and explain his margin system.

To record a death properly he has to witness it. A completionist with an unfinishable list begins arranging to be present, and then arranging more often. His archive is the only accurate record of the era, which makes the best historical source in the world a trophy list — and makes it useful to Mors, because it is a copy of the memory that was taken. The cure was assembled out of murders.

**Nine Tenths.** A butcher who is his own stock. Nothing ends, so he eats himself, and he comes back, and he sells the difference. He has prices. He has a preferred cut. He will tell the player which part of him is best this season and he will not be wrong.

He has never eaten anyone else. He is firm about it and genuinely offended by the suggestion — one scruple, kept scrupulously, inside a practice with no other scruples available. It hurts every time, as much as the first. He has priced that in.

The act is never shown. What the player sees is a counter, a sign, and the stock.

The Low Court would like him ended: a mortal who has made himself unfinishable is the abomination they were founded to hunt, and he is a tradesman in a cave. How the player treats him is how the Court first takes an interest (§XVII).

Both of them die when Death remembers. Neither is a villain, and the game never says which way mercy runs.

---

## §XV. Mors as a Wandering Figure

Mors should not behave like a conventional town NPC or ordinary boss.

He is a **wandering encounter**.

He appears in places touched by death:

- caves
- graveyards
- battlefields
- the Underworld
- ruins
- wilderness
- places where someone has recently died

He is taller than the player, roughly four to five blocks in apparent height, with subtly incorrect proportions. He should look almost ordinary at first glance, but never entirely right.

### How He Looks

**[LOCKED — the read. The sprite is built.]**

Drawn in three layers over one canvas: body, cloak, head. The body is still and faces
wherever he was put; the cloak hangs over it and stirs with the weather, ripple in a calm
and a thirty-degree lean downwind in a gale; only the head turns, and it turns to follow
the nearest living person, tilting up and down to find their face and giving up the crane
when they are too far overhead.

The effect wanted is a man who has been standing there a while and has noticed you. He
does not walk, gesture or emote. He does not display a name over his head and hostile
things do not spawn near him (Stillness), so the ground around him is quiet in a way the
player will feel before they can say why.

### The Two Wanderers

**[LOCKED]**

He and Pugream (§XXXI) are both wanderers, and that is the point of the pair rather than an
accident of it. They walk for opposite reasons.

Pugream walks toward something. He is carrying his teacher's sword to wherever the two of
them were going, he may never arrive, and he keeps walking. Mors walks without a
destination, because he goes where death has been, which is everywhere, and he has
forgotten the errand.

Hence the presentation. The player never sees Mors travel: he is only ever already there,
standing in a place he cannot account for. Pugream is only ever seen in transit, arriving
or leaving. Same fact about both men, shown as opposites — one is going somewhere, the
other has forgotten where he was going — and both would tell you they are only passing
through.

The look follows from that and not the other way round. Mors is too tall, proportioned
slightly wrong, cloaked, obscured, and does not move. Pugream is the control sample:
ordinary height, correctly proportioned, plainly dressed, drawn mid-stride.

**[PROPOSAL] If the two of them ever meet.** Pugream introduces himself every time, to
everyone, because he assumes he has been forgotten (§XXXI). Mors forgets everyone. Neither
is troubled by the other. The man who expects to be forgotten meets the god who cannot
help it, the exchange is entirely unremarkable to both of them, and it should be the worst
thing in the book to watch.

He can be kind, confused, strangely lucid, or quietly terrifying.

Possible dialogue:

> “You've been hurt. Don't worry. It happens.”

> “You died yesterday, didn't you?”

> “No?”

> “I'm sorry. I must be thinking of someone else.”

> “I know you.”

> “I seem to have lost track of myself again.”

> “They aren't dead yet.”

Mors may disappear without the normal mechanics of an NPC leaving or dying.

He does not need to be fought in ordinary progression.

### The Secret Confrontation

There may be a hidden questline in which the player can choose to confront Mors.

This confrontation must **not** mean that the player simply kills the concept of Death.

Death is fundamental to reality.

The encounter should instead concern Mors's broken identity, his memory, and the consequences of the Archmage's intervention.

## §XVI. The Death Daggers

**[LOCKED — three words, three levels]**

- **Death Dagger** — the class of object. Mors had several. They are the ceremonial instruments of §VII; Birth stole one; the player finds one.
- **Blade of Mors** — the item. The specific Death Dagger the player carries.
- **Quietus** — the act. What a Death Dagger does when it works: it finishes something already dying, so that it stays dead. Never said in-game as a mechanic name until it happens; the Low Court's word for it.

"Death Dagger" is the plain name and the one the world uses. "Quietus" is the old word for a debt discharged, and belongs to the people who keep accounts.

### What a Death Dagger Is

A Death Dagger does not kill. It **finishes** — and only what someone entitled has already begun. Death, or the maker.

That is why Birth's stolen one works: she is the maker of what she ends. And it is why the player's does not: Death has forgotten how to begin anything (§VIII), so a Death Dagger in a mortal's hand is a knife with nobody behind it. For most of the game the Blade of Mors is exactly that. Its weakness is not a balance apology. It is the state of the world.

> *A blade meant not to kill, but to finish what death had already begun.*  
> *Its former bearer walked beside the dying, carrying no banner and seeking no glory.*  
> *Strangely, none remember his name.*

### Where It Was

The Blade stands driven into an ash plateau at the bottom of the world — the Mors Monument — with fireblossoms and ash trees grown up around it. Nobody built the monument.

After the Archmage's tampering (§VIII), Mors walked beside the dying to the lowest place there is and drove the blade into the ground **the way a man drives a cane down to keep his feet**. Then he walked on without it, because he had forgotten it was what was holding him up. The blade marks the exact place he first did not know who he was. The trees came because that is what grows where death was done (§XXVIII), and the world kept it for him.

So the most important object in the world was not arranged for the player (§III). It **leaked**: the one person who should have kept it is the one who cannot remember it matters. It is not weird that a nobody carries it. It is weird that it was left — and the game never explains that. The ruin does.

### When It Wakes

**[LOCKED]** Not until Mors remembers (§XXXII.3, post-Moon Lord). Then the Blade the player has carried since pre-Hell becomes what it was. Quietus is the strike, not a new item: the Blade's existing release becomes it. On anything already dying — below a life threshold, §XVI's "near an inevitable end" made literal — it does not deal damage; it ends. On a boss, only in the last sliver — and it is the only way a god is felled for good rather than resummoned. Everything else the player kills still comes back. A thing Quietus'd does not.

**Cost.** §XVIII: exceptions cost someone who did not consent. The Low Court has wanted this knife for centuries and never held one. The first Quietus is the moment they learn the player has one — the same shape as the Orb alerting the Sky (§IX) — and is what turns the Court toward the player. It also feeds the Death ending (§XXXII.3): the deathless thing that can end others is precisely the abomination the Court was founded to hunt.

**It does nothing to Mors.** Death cannot die is the one hard rule. In that fight the knife simply fails, and he notices the player tried.

The player may also take Birth's dagger off her. It does not work for them either, for the same reason. Two Death Daggers in the game, neither of which works for a mortal until Death does.

# PART IV — THE ORDERS

*Mortals who organised around the gods: to judge them, to inherit them, or to build past them.*

---

## §XVII. The Low Court

**[LOCKED — replaces the working name "Godslayers"]**

A court that sits beneath the world and tries what is above it. "Low" means underground, humble, and illegitimate all at once, and the name answers their own founding line:

> **A god that cannot die cannot be judged.**

So they convened a court.

They are small, secretive, and **still operating**. The player will meet roughly five of them across the whole game.

### What They Know, and How

Their knowledge is inherited, not revealed. The Lords (§XII) and the Kingdom of Grace (§XIX) kept records of what gods are and how they behave, and the Low Court is what is left of the people who read them.

This gives them their shape: **founded out of an aristocracy of knowledge, staffed now by ordinary people.** The library is gone. The work continues.

### Structure

**Five people matter.** The rest are periphery, and the periphery holds no doctrine at all.

Ordinary followers are ordinary. They have no position on Death, no theory of anchors, no oath. What they have is a **debt**. Somebody settled something for them once — paid off a creditor, hid a brother, buried a child properly — and now they keep a drawer, carry a letter, or look away at the right moment. They do not ask what is in the letter.

This is why a body that large never leaks: **you cannot confess a doctrine you were never told.**

**The Tax Collector is a follower** at this tier. **[LOCKED]** He keeps accounts, he came back from Hell, and he is rude enough that nobody befriends him, which is the best cover there is. He never confirms it. He says one thing he should not know, and if the player returns carrying the right object — the Blade, or later the Orb — he says one more.

### They Have Never Killed a God

This is the fact the whole order is built on top of and never says out loud.

Their evidence that gods can be felled is a single attempt: **they poisoned the Jungle** in order to wound the God of Life. It was built rather than cursed — pumps, vats, pipes, an industry run for as long as it took — and that is how the player tells their work from the infectious absolutes of §XXVI. Metaphysical forces do not leave machinery. It did not kill her. Life answered the way Life answers everything —

> **She adapted.**

The Jungle came back stronger by an order of magnitude and has not stopped since. Everything that makes the Jungle the most hostile place in the world is that answer, still running. **Plantera reads as the organ Life grew to give it** — dormant, deep, triggered by disturbance. **Chlorophyte is the antibody in mineral form**, a metal the Jungle learned to make because something was eating it.

So the Low Court did not injure Life. They pushed her past her boundary, which is exactly what §I calls monstrous. Their one operation *created* the thing it was meant to prevent.

And their doctrine rests on a misreading: they hurt a god, the god reacted, and they took a reaction for a vulnerability.

### Doctrine as It Stands Now

> **Other gods can be felled. Even Death can be contained.**

Note that the creed has already drifted and nobody has admitted it. They founded on *judging* gods. They now say felled, and contained.

### Two Things They Are Wrong About

**One.** Their premise is that a god who cannot die cannot be judged. Death cannot die (§VIII) — and Death is the most thoroughly punished thing in the world. Mors *was* judged, not by a crusade but by one wizard with good intentions, and the sentence is still being served. The proof they have spent generations hunting walks past their camps asking people where the dead go, and they do not recognise him.

**Two.** Containing Death is §XXXII.4 — nothing ends, existence crowds up with things that should have stopped. Pursued honestly, their project delivers the worst outcome in the book. They would be doing on purpose, at scale, what the Archmage did once by accident.

### The Heretic

There is no schism and no second faction. One court, one doctrine — and **one member who has privately worked out what a contained Death actually produces**, and concluded the only honest version is the opposite: heal Mors, let real death return, and accept that it comes for him too.

He cannot say it out loud. He has not got the nerve to be a heretic yet.

### Their Methods

They have committed atrocities in pursuit of this belief:

- sacrificing mortals
- kidnapping divine vessels
- dissecting celestial creatures
- experimenting upon divine essence
- provoking gods deliberately
- studying divine anatomy
- developing methods of killing gods

They also deface what accepts worship. Chipped faces on angel statues, scratched-out names on shrines — their signature is in the world long before the player knows there is an order to attach it to.

### Divine Anchors

The Low Court discovered that gods possess **anchors** tying their existence to reality.

Possible examples:

- **Mors → Memory**
- **Life → Living organisms**
- **Birth → Creation**
- **Loi → Sky / Order**

Destroying an Anchor may allow a god to truly perish.

However, they eventually confront a distinction:

> **There are gods. And then there is Death.**

Death is not simply another divine monarch that can be assassinated.

### How the Player Meets Them

**[LOCKED]**

They never ambush anybody. Ambush is not their register (§IV) and they have never needed it.

Their mark is in the world from the first hour — chipped faces on angel statues, names scratched off shrines — long before there is an order to attach it to. The altar the orb sits in carries it (§XXX). The Tax Collector says the one thing he should not know. A courier lies dead on a road with a sealed letter in a script the player cannot read. And after the first Quietus (§XVI), one of the five simply meets the player: not hostile, interested, offering something genuinely useful that costs somebody else.

The escalation is not danger. It is that every important room the player walks into has a chair already pulled out.

---

## §XVIII. How a God Is Killed

**[LOCKED — the method, and the register]**

Nobody in this world out-fights a god. A god is killed by finding the rule that makes it unkillable and breaking that instead. The weapon is never power. It is an **exception**, and it is usually stolen, usually a fragment, and it usually costs someone who did not consent.

This has a consequence the Low Court has not solved.

Death has forgotten how to end things (§VIII). So the only objects left in the world that can still *end* anything are **the Death Daggers** (§XVI) — the ceremonial instruments of §VII, one of which Birth already stole. What they do, the Court calls a **Quietus**.

> **A blade meant not to kill, but to finish what death had already begun.**

That is the only true god-killing tool in existence, and there are very few of them. Everything the Low Court has built is a substitute for one. It is why the Jungle only got angry.

Acquiring a dagger is the endgame of the order, whether or not they can say so.

---

## §XIX. The Kingdom of Grace

A major royal civilization known as the **Kingdom of Grace** existed in the ancient world.

The kingdom's legitimacy was intertwined with divinity.

“Grace” can simultaneously mean:

- divine favor
- mercy
- elegance
- royal dignity
- an undeserved gift
- divine legitimacy

The phrase **“May I say grace?”** can therefore carry both innocent and sinister meanings.

#### Shimmer and Grace

**[PROPOSAL / DEVELOPING]**

The royal civilization may have discovered Shimmer and mistaken it for divine Grace.

The royal bloodline could therefore have been transformed by Shimmer rather than genuinely blessed by a god.

This would make the kingdom's sacred legitimacy a historical misunderstanding—or perhaps a deliberate lie.

#### The Sample

**[PROPOSAL — the timing is what makes it work]**

The one seam of it in the world comes up under their capital, and the whole city is
built on top of that (`PAD_World_Plan.md`). They did not find it and then build there.
They built there because it was there.

Note where the lock falls in the order. Grace's good years are **before** the working;
the Archmage locked Shimmer **after** it (§XI). So the kingdom was drinking the live
thing, the whole time, and whatever the bloodline became it actually became.

The favour was real. It simply was not divine, and nobody has been able to tell the
difference in three hundred years because the difference was drained out of it by a man
who never came to the table.

What is down there now is a sample. Kept, in the sense that somebody kept it — the
ewers are still on the trolley and the procession route is still worn into the floor.
It is water.

**[OPEN]** Who kept it, once it stopped being anything. Habit is the dull answer and
probably the right one.

### Lord Bustworth

**[LOCKED — the man and the acts. His fate is open; see the timeline.]**

The last head of the Kingdom of Grace, and the reason there is not a later one.

He was not the heir by any reading the kingdom would have accepted. He took the kingdom
by killing his father, and he did it with warning: he had a standing connection to the
Arcanum (§XXIV), and somebody inside it knew what the Archmage was working on and told
him what was coming.

It is worth being plain about the order of those two facts, because the whole character
sits on it. He did not kill his father under cover of the amnesty. He killed him
**before** it, knowing the amnesty was coming.

#### The Patricide

**[LOCKED]**

A killing that landed in the last years when killing still held.

> **He spent the last of permanence on his own father.**

The reasoning is sound and that is the horror of it. A crown taken after the working
could be reclaimed by the man it was taken from, because nothing stays dead any more.
Taken before, it could not. He was told the window was closing and he used it.

It also leaves him in a position no one else in the world occupies: he is a man who took
a life while a life could still be taken, and then watched the world stop charging for
it. His crime was refunded. Whether he was glad of that is exactly the thing the player
should not be told.

#### The Title

**[LOCKED]**

He never called himself king, and could not have. The crown of Grace rests on divine
favour (§XIX above), and a man who kills his father cannot stand under that word without
somebody eventually saying so.

So he declined the title he had no claim to and invented one he could give himself, and
chose a grander-sounding word for a lesser office: **Lord**. Lord of Grace.

The pun is not accidental and the kingdom could not unhear it. Grace means an undeserved
gift. He gave himself one and put it on the letterhead. Every time the old blessing was
asked at his table — *may I say grace?* — it was a small trial, and he won it every time,
because nobody would say the other thing out loud.

#### The Good Years

**[LOCKED — he must not be a cartoon]**

He was good at it.

He was clever, he was physically formidable, and the kingdom prospered under him in a way
it had not under the bloodline that had the right to it. Roads, grain, order, work. The
best ruler Grace ever had was the one with no claim to rule.

The player should meet the prosperity before the murder. Find the good years first — a
kingdom plainly well run — and learn later what it cost and who paid it. Presented the
other way round it is a villain's backstory, and he is not that.

#### The Goblin War

**[LOCKED — the war and its outcome. Its dates are open.]**

The goblins came, and their engineering was simply better than his (§XXI).

Grace's claim was favour and what beat it was a better trap. That is the right way for
this kingdom to fall and it is the book's whole thesis in one campaign: steel over
blessing, workmanship over legitimacy, and nothing in its prime (§I).

What the amnesty does to that war is the part worth holding onto. Once nothing stays
dead, a war cannot be lost — only failed to be won. So Grace did not fall to goblin
engineering in any afternoon that could be pointed at. It bled out over a very long time
under a lord who would not concede a fight that had stopped being able to end, and who
was, in the narrowest sense, still winning when there was nobody left to win it.

The ruins the player walks through are not a defeat. They are a stalemate that nobody was
ever allowed to call.

---

#### The Cupbearer

**[LOCKED — that she is there, what she was, and why she is small. The name, the pour and
the fight are PROPOSAL.]**

There is a small dragon in the Font, and she has been there since before the Working.

She was the one who carried it. Three hundred years of ewers up the shaft to the table,
along a route worn into the floor by her own feet, and what was left in the jug was hers
because it was going to be poured away.

The kingdom drank the live thing on purpose and called it a god's favour. She drank what
was left and called it nothing. The favour was real — that is settled (§XIX, the Shimmer
reading, and `PAD_World_Plan.md` §4a). It simply was not divine, and a substance does not
check the guest list. So the claim came true, in the cellar, on the one person in the
building who was not making it.

**She is small, and that is the whole of her.** The question was locked while she was
still answering it. She stopped where she stood: not a hatchling and not a dragon, a
thing three hundred years into a change with most of the way still to run and no way left
to run it. Armageddon (§IX), which the Pilgrim brings the player to later, is three times
her size and worse. Nothing in this world is in its prime (§I), and she is the plainest
case in the book.

**Her fire is gold and it is weak.** Gold because that is the colour the kingdom put on
everything it claimed. Weak because that is what the claim was worth. The breath is not
the fight.

**The magic is not hers.** She is standing in the last live Shimmer in the world and it
still asks its question through her, in small ways, at short range. She is the only thing
left that can change what a thing *is*, and she does not know that this is remarkable; she
has always been able to do it. That is why she is the first fight after the Moon Lord. The
player comes down here looking for evidence that the live thing ever existed (§XI), and
finds it keeping house.

**She is not guarding anything.** She is on shift. The last order she was given came from
somebody three hundred years dead and nobody has been down to tell her the kingdom fell.
She fights the player the way a servant fights a stranger who has walked into the pantry.

**[PROPOSAL] The pour.** The trolley is still set and the ewers are clean, all of them,
kept. When the player comes in she fills one and sets it out, and then the fight starts.
The liquid is inert, the courtesy means nothing, and she has no way of knowing either.

**[PROPOSAL]** She says one thing, once, and it is not about the player.

##### What She Looks Like

**[LOCKED]**

Her claws are pearl and they give off light. They cut like obsidian and they do not chip.
Everything about her hands is treasury.

Her hide is wrinkled and tough and the colour of nothing.

That is the whole character before a word is said. The kingdom's wealth grew on the parts
of her that did the work — the claws that held the ewers, three hundred years of them — and
stopped there. The rest of her is a working animal that has been on her feet a long time.
The player is meant to see the hands first and read wealth, and then see the rest.

##### What Her Eyes Do

**[LOCKED — the precognition, the dodge and the teleport. The tell is PROPOSAL.]**

She sees what has not happened yet.

Standing half-changed is standing between one thing and another, and what is not yet is
the same country as what something could be instead. The locked question leaks out of her
through the eyes. Nobody in the book says so.

**She is the only boss in the game that dodges a projectile.** Not a dash on a timer — she
moves because of the shot, before the shot. She also steps out of the world and back into
it somewhere else.

In a mod named for pistols and daggers, the first fight after the Moon Lord is the one the
pistol cannot hit. That is the argument the fight makes and it does not need a line of
dialogue: the answer is to get close, or to fill a space rather than aim at a point.
Foresight has no reply to *nowhere to go*.

**[PROPOSAL] The tell.** Her eyes go to where the player is about to fire, before the
player fires. It is the only warning and it is enough.

And she has seen everything that was going to happen for three hundred years and she is in
a cellar, on shift. The book never remarks on this.

##### How It Ends

**[LOCKED]**

Her fire is weak and the fight is long, and weak fire applied for long enough takes a
room. She burns her own floor out from under herself. Nobody does it to her.

Her wings go through. At the end she cannot hold herself up, the floor she has spent the
fight destroying gives way over the shaft head, and she falls the length of her own shaft
into the Shimmer.

She burns deeply.

Her form goes out of shape and into the dimension, and the pool closes over nothing.

**Do not explain the pool.** The lock is on and what is under it is a liquid of mystery.
The kingdom carried it to the table for three hundred years and none of them asked
(§XIX, the shaft-head inscription), and this book does not ask either. It is not water,
it is not a god in hiding, it is not a reagent with a stated effect. It is the thing
nobody knows about, still not known about, doing something to somebody.

She is not dead. Nothing is, in this era (§XIV) — but she is not one of the *overstayed*
either, because there is nothing left to come back together. Dead is a condition available
in this world. She is not in it. She is not in this one.

What is left in the room: a burnt floor with a hole in it, and the trolley, and on the
trolley one ewer she poured before the fight, still full.

Deliberately unresolved:

- Where she went.
- Whether it finished the change, or only ended the question.
- Whether she knew the kingdom was gone.
- Whether she saw all of this coming, and how long ago.

#### Marriage Ending

It represents choosing:

The ancient problems remain.

## §XXI. The Engineering Traditions

The Mechanic is not the world's only engineer.

Different civilizations developed different technological traditions.

### Ancient Engineers

Prehistoric engineers whose technology is so advanced that later civilizations mistake it for magic.

### Goblin Artificers

A tradition of crude, ingenious mechanical invention.

They specialize in:

- traps
- mechanisms
- weapons
- improvised engineering
- reforging

The Goblin Tinkerer descends from this tradition.

#### The Goblin Empire

**[LOCKED]**

The tradition produced an empire, and the empire fought the Kingdom of Grace (§XIX) and
out-built it.

Their conduct in that war is the reason the Artificer tradition is not simply the
charming one. They used their machines to manufacture terror rather than casualties, and
they used civilians to develop them. Not as collateral and not in anger: as subjects, on
a programme, with notes kept.

Note what the amnesty does to a programme like that. A subject who cannot die is a
subject with no disposal problem, no shortage, and no end to a session. Every natural
limit such work has is a limit made of death, and there was not any.

**[OPEN — the timing, and it changes the temperature]** Whether the programme ran before
the working, after it, or straddled it. The strongest version straddles: a record whose
tone changes on one particular day, when the people keeping it noticed the rules had
moved, and did not stop.

The facility is a place the player can stand inside, in the manner of the Jungle
apparatus: built things leave machinery, and machinery can be walked through and worked
out. Nothing in it is labelled.

**[PROPOSAL] The tally-plate.** §XIV gives the world recut gravestones — one name, four
death-dates, each struck out and carved again beneath the last, because somebody kept
paying the mason. Put the same object inside the facility: a goblin plate listing
subjects by number, one number struck through and rewritten under itself over and over in
the same careful hand. The player has already seen that shape in a churchyard and felt
sorry about it. Here it means the opposite thing and they are left to work that out.

#### Why the Tinkerer Left

**[LOCKED]**

He was one of the ones who objected, and when objecting did not work he left the empire.

This is the cause of a thing the book already locked. §XXI has him as the man who says
*there are things you shouldn't do*, and as the Mechanic's lover who does not try to fix
her but understands her. He understands her because he has already watched *if it works,
it was worth making* run to its conclusion, in his own empire, on people, and he walked
out over it.

He is not squeamish. He is the only person in the world arguing from the far side of that
question.

Vanilla finds him bound in a cave, exiled by his own army for losing a battle. That is
what his army says.

**[OPEN]** His one line he should not know — the shape the Tax Collector's line uses. He
will not say what he saw, and the player should be able to tell that the refusal is not
modesty.

### The Mechanic

The greatest modern engineer specializing in **autonomous machines and adaptive machinery**.

She is brilliant but morally compromised.

Her philosophy is approximately:

> **If it works, it was worth making.**

She is willing to experiment with:

- souls
- biological material
- divine essence
- autonomous weapons
- god-killing machines
- ancient technology

Her tragedy is not that she is secretly evil.

It is that she believes understanding something gives her the right to manipulate it.

#### Mechanic & Goblin Tinkerer

The Mechanic and Goblin Tinkerer are lovers.

Their relationship is genuine.

They have opposing philosophies:

**Mechanic:**  
> If it can be done, why shouldn't it be done?

**Tinkerer:**  
> Because there are things you shouldn't do.

He does not simply “fix” her.

He understands her.

### Firearms

**[LOCKED — the frame; no first gun]**

The dagger's verb is *finish* (§XVI). The gun's verb is **stop**.

A Death Dagger closes a death already begun, and you must be close. A gun begins a death nobody was entitled to begin, from a distance, without asking. It exists because of the Archmage: it is the first weapon invented in a world where nothing stays dead, and it was never built to end anything. It was built to make a thing stop long enough.

Its symbols follow from that. **The report** — a dagger tells only the one it is used on; a gun tells the whole world something happened, the same noise the Orb makes at the Sky. **The casing** — brass left where it fell, a receipt for a decision; the Low Court keeps accounts, and their ruins should have casings in them. **The trigger** — one finger, one choice, made alone, with no rite and no witness.

The tradition: the Goblin Artificers made the first one (a trap you carry). The Mechanic finished the thought — a gun that fires without a hand. The Megashark (§XXII) is the end of that line, and *Handswitch* is the moment the hand left the loop.

Every gun in the mod takes something from the wielder (Handswitch takes life). A substitute for a permission nobody has is never free.

**There is no first gun as an object.** It is mentioned once, by the Goblin Tinkerer, and never again:

> *"The first one wasn't for killing. It was for a thing you couldn't walk up to. Then somebody walked up to something anyway."*

#### Mechanic's Death

**[LOCKED — timing and cause]**

The Mechanic dies **after Mors remembers** (§XXXII.3) — post-Moon Lord, at the end of the arc.

She is not killed and she is not punished. When Death's memory comes back, death starts working again, and everyone who had been living on the amnesia stops. Hers is one of those. It simply becomes possible, and then it happens.

The framing is tragedy and consequence rather than punishment or romanticized sacrifice. She is not the only one; she is the one the player knows.

A possible catalyst remains that she creates or encounters something she realizes she **cannot control, understand, or repair** (§XXII is the obvious candidate).

She works it out before the player does. She has the Blade's measure, she knows what the restoration costs, and she knows that a world where endings land is a world she is not in. She hands the work over anyway, and not nobly.

> **If it works, it was worth making was never a rule about machines.**

# PART V — THE WORLD

*The places and systems the player stands in, and what each of them is evidence of.*

## §XXVI. The Three Infectious Forces

The Corruption, Crimson, and Hallow are not simply morally aligned biomes.

They are ancient **infectious metaphysical forces** capable of overwriting reality.

An ancient civilization created a **Seal** to suppress all three.

They represent different absolutes.

---

### Corruption — The Absolute of Decay

The Corruption takes the necessity of endings and pushes it beyond reason.

Its principle:

> **Everything must end.**

Taken to its extreme:

> **Everything must end immediately.**

Matter rots. Identity erodes. Boundaries collapse through decay.

It is not merely evil.

It is **ending without proportion**.

---

### Crimson — The Absolute of Life

The Crimson takes biological life and pushes it toward total assimilation.

Its principle:

> **Everything must live.**

Taken to its extreme:

> **Everything must become one living thing.**

Individuality disappears.

Flesh becomes ecosystem.

Life becomes indistinguishable from consumption and reproduction.

---

### Hallow — The Absolute of Perfection

The Hallow takes purity and beauty beyond moderation.

Its principle:

> **Everything must be perfect.**

Anything imperfect is corrected.

The Hallow is especially dangerous because it may appear benevolent, beautiful, and holy.

Its horror is **perfection without mercy**.

---

### Why They Cannot Share a World

> Everything must end.
> Everything must live.
> Everything must be perfect.

No two of those can both be true, and none of the three will take second place. Set any two of them side by side and each begins eating the other. Rot arrives, flesh grows over it, light corrects them both, and the rot takes the light because the light is a thing and things end.

That is not a flaw in them. It is the only property of theirs that anybody has ever been able to use.

---

### The Three Holds

**[LOCKED — the player is never told any of this]**

The Seal is the second work. The first one is still in the ground, and the player walks over all three pieces of it long before going down to the fire.

The houses began by holding the three apart, one method each, because no two of them could be held the same way. Each method was chosen for what the force in it refuses.

**One is pressed.** An evil put down under the rock and packed in from every side, with nothing left to spread into. A thing whose principle is that everything must end cannot be walled, because a wall is a thing and things end. So they gave it nothing to end. The shell around it is not a container. It is the absence of anywhere to go.

**One is warded.** Nothing was built around the Hallow. Anything built is imperfect and what is imperfect gets corrected, which the houses learned by losing two attempts to their own masonry. The third attempt used no stone. They put the hold into rock that was already there and left it looking like rock.

**One is left breathing.** The third was not held at all. It was given the open air, a shore to sit on, and the slow leave to spread. Three absolutes wear each other down only while all three are pushing, and a force with nothing to push against stops pushing. What stops pushing cannot be used to hold anything down.

> **None of the three can be killed, because the lock needs all three.**

The houses did not spare them. They could not afford a dead one.

The first work leaks, and the leak was accepted. A spreading evil on the surface is the price of a lock with all its sides.

What the first work taught them is the whole of the second. Held apart, each of the three is only itself, and only itself is enough to get out. Held **against** each other, each one is busy. The Wall is the three of them put in one room with nothing in between, which is the only arrangement in which none of them wins.

**What the player finds:** three of them in one world, which is not supposed to happen, and three different kinds of containment around them, and no explanation anywhere. The pressed one has a shell with fingers in the rock. The warded one has no shell at all. The third one has a beach.

Deliberately unresolved:

- Which of the two evils was chosen to breathe, and whether it was chosen or only the one they failed to catch.
- Whether the houses laid the three holds, or inherited them and put their names to the work.
- What was in the two attempts the Hallow corrected.

---

## §XXVII. The Seal and the Wall of Flesh

The ancient Seal exists beneath the world, deep within the Underworld.

Its purpose is to suppress the three infectious forces.

The **Wall of Flesh is the living manifestation and guardian of the Seal.**

It is not simply guarding Hell.

It is guarding the world above from what the Seal contains.

When the player kills the Wall:

> **The Seal breaks.**

Hardmode begins.

The Corruption, Crimson, and Hallow are unleashed to spread through the world.

The player therefore unknowingly destroys an ancient containment system.

The Wall is not necessarily evil.

It is performing a function that the modern world has forgotten.

**The Underworld is not an afterlife.** It is a real physical region beneath the world, with its own history and civilizations.

---

### What the Wall Is Made Of

**[LOCKED]**

The houses could not build a lock out of nothing. They built it out of what it holds.

> **The Wall of Flesh is the three forces themselves, bound into one body.**

Corruption's rot, Crimson's meat, Hallow's light — three absolutes forced into a single shape and set against one another, each refusing the other two, none able to win. The lock is a deadlock. That is why the thing is a wall of mouths and eyes and running flesh: it is not a creature the houses made, it is three infections holding each other down.

The three holds above ground (§XXVI, *The Three Holds*) are what they tried first, and the Wall is what they built after that failed. Same three forces, same houses, opposite idea: stop keeping them apart and put them in one room.

It also explains what killing it does. The player does not open a door. The player ends a stalemate, and all three go free in the same second, everywhere at once.

---

### The Learned Houses and the Key

The civilization that laid the Seal was not a kingdom of soldiers.

It was an aristocracy of knowledge — learned houses that studied the three forces closely enough to bind them, and understood that a lock nobody could open was only a delay.

So they wrote the key into themselves.

> **The key to the Seal is a bloodline, not an object.**

It was written into the houses so that it could be inherited but never stolen. A key that is carried cannot be taken from a vault.

The houses are gone. The line is not.

**The Guide is the last of it — or the last who does not know that he is.**

He was raised on old books and half-remembered stories, the kind told to make a small boy feel important. He does not know what he carries. He is beginning to suspect.

The Guide Voodoo Doll is a likeness of the key. Demons carry them because they want the Seal open. Burning the effigy in the fire beneath the world is how the lock is turned — **the Wall answers to the key's death, not to the doll.**

> *"The one it resembles has not seen it since he was small."*

When the player kills the Wall, they have used him. The Seal breaks, and everything above follows.

Deliberately unresolved:

- Whether the houses meant the key to be used, or only kept.
- Whether the Guide's death breaks the Seal, or merely opens it.
- Whether there are other lines.

### The Quiet Work

**[LOCKED — this is what joins the two catastrophes]**

The Seal was not announced. The houses built it in secret and swore the work out of the record, and the secrecy was not modesty. It was the second lock. What nobody knows about, nobody tests; a bound thing the world has forgotten is bound twice.

That half of the bargain is still holding. It held long after the flesh stopped being the only problem.

The houses did not do it alone. They brought in a young scholar from outside — not a member, not sworn, a specialist consulted on the binding of things that cannot be killed. He did the work he was asked for and he left. What he took with him was the method (§XIII).

So the two catastrophes in this book are one catastrophe. The Seal taught the Archmage how to break the rule that holds a thing together; he went and broke the rule that holds endings together; and everyone who could have recognised what he had done was sworn to silence about the only comparable working.

**What the player finds:** a correspondence in two halves. His side is in the Arcanum ruins (§XXIV). Theirs is beneath the world. Neither half names him. Together they do.

And the release is worse than it was built to be. Three absolutes wear each other down only if wearing down is available, and since the amnesty it has not been. What comes out of the broken Seal cannot be exhausted. Hardmode is an old plan meeting a wound its architects never heard about.

---

## §XXVIII. The Fungal Caves

The Fungal Caves are an ancient subterranean ecosystem.

Their great fungal networks operate through:

> **Death → decomposition → nutrients → new life.**

Unlike the Crimson, fungi do not reject death.

They require it.

The fungal ecosystem therefore exists in strange harmony with the proper function of Death.

The giant mushrooms are fruiting bodies of immense interconnected mycelial networks.

### Where Endings Still Land

**[LOCKED]**

Fungi do not reject death. They require it, and a network built on endings is the one system in the world that never stopped working when Death forgot.

So the fungal caves are the only place where things finish. Everywhere else the dead lie intact in the fields they fell in; down here they go back into the ground on schedule and the mushrooms grow on what is done with. It is the last honest ecology in the world.

That is why the network remembers the man who came asking where the dead go, and why nobody above ground does.

### Truffle

Truffle is a sapient member of this fungal ecosystem.

He may know things that ordinary surface dwellers cannot because the fungal network retains impressions of what has happened around it.

#### Truffle and Mors

**[PROPOSAL]**

Truffle may recognize Mors indirectly through the memories of the fungal network.

Possible dialogue:

> “The mushrooms know him.”

> “He asked me where the dead go.”

> “I told him.”

> “He didn't remember.”

This makes the fungal ecosystem an unexpected witness to Mors's broken identity.

## §XXIX. Player Flight

The player cannot truly fly before defeating the **Empress of Light**.

After defeating her, the player gains limited access to flight.

This can be incorporated into the mythology of Loi.

Early game:

> **You walk beneath the heavens.**

After Empress:

> **You may briefly enter them.**

Eventually:

> **You no longer ask the sky for permission.**

Flight therefore becomes more than a mobility upgrade.

It represents the player's gradual transition from **mortal subject beneath the heavens** to someone capable of directly challenging the authority of the Sky.

## §XXX. The Orb — Grace, and the Question

**[LOCKED — implemented as the Ominous Orb / Legends mode]**

The old kingdom found Shimmer and took it for Grace (§XIX).

It had the Gemmasons (§XXIII) cut a vessel for one drop of it: a gem hollowed to hold what the court believed was a blessing. It was kept under glass and carried at coronations. To the kingdom it was the holiest thing it owned — proof that the line was chosen.

Shimmer's question is *what if you were something else* (§XI).

Put to a person, it makes them something else.

Put to a god, it strips the vessel. The avatar answers, and what stands there is what the god actually is.

### How the Low Court Has It

They did not steal it. Their knowledge comes out of the Lords and Grace's own records (§XVII), which makes them the heirs of the people who catalogued the thing in the first place. A fallen aristocracy's relic, kept by the ordinary people who inherited the work but not the library.

### What Breaking It Does

Broken on iron, on a mortal's tool, the question is asked out loud and the whole world hears it.

**It alerts the heavens.**

Shimmer's question is *what if you were something else*. Loi's law is that nothing beneath the heavens may defy the heavens (§IX). Asking the world Shimmer's question in the open is the loudest heresy available, and the sky answers by tightening. Everything gets harder — not because the world was broken, but because the world is now **being corrected**. It is being watched.

> **Made to hold a blessing. Broken to ask a question.**

This is deliberately *not* a second world-break. The Wall (§XXVII) ends a stalemate and frees three forces. The orb changes nothing about the world's structure — it changes who is looking at it. Attention, not damage.

The Guide's "intangible ominous presence" after the orb is broken is exactly that.

In play: the orb is set on an anvil and broken with a hammer. There is no way back. Mechanically this is the mod's difficulty mode; in fiction it is a god's attention, and the Low Court arranged for the player to have the orb.

Deliberately unresolved:

- Whether the kingdom knew what it was keeping.
- Whether a world that has answered can ever stop.
- What Loi does about it beyond tightening.

## §XXXI. Pugream, the Pilgrim

**[LOCKED — name and kit]**

His name is **Pugream**. A near miss of pilgrim, close enough that most of the world hears the word it is not, and the first syllable is *pupil*: he was somebody's student, and the name is the clue. One NPC calls him "the pupil" once, so the reading is available.

He carries a **greatbow**, and that is what he is. Distance is his whole character — he helps without arriving. He finishes the Eye with an arrow from off-screen; he gives berries and rum and leaves.

On his back, wrapped, is a **great sword** too big for him. Everyone assumes it is his. It is not — it was his teacher's, and he is carrying it to wherever they were going. He never draws it, until the one boss he fights beside the player, and it is clear in two swings that it was never meant for his hands. After that fight it is on the player's back, or on his grave.

Guts' sword and Igon's bow in one man, without making him a kit: the bow is what he *is*; the sword is what he is *carrying*.

### How He Looks

**[LOCKED — the read. The sprite is not drawn.]**

Exactly player height and exactly correctly proportioned: he is the control sample for
Mors (§XV), and the player should feel the difference between the two without being told.
They are the book's two wanderers and they walk for opposite reasons; §XV, The Two
Wanderers, holds the pair.

One thing about his outline is wrong, and only one. The wrapped sword across his back is
too long for him, slung at an angle nobody would draw from, overhanging his shoulder and
his hip. A man carrying something that is not his, readable at a glance. Everything else
about him is regular.

He has no colour identity, because he belongs to nobody: unbleached wool, dun, oiled
brown leather, road grey. No sigil, no heraldry, no order's mark — in a world where every
faction signs its work, the one man who wants nothing signs nothing. Spend the single
saturated colour in his palette on the cord binding the sword, in a shade matching nothing
else he wears, because somebody else tied it.

His kit is **repaired, not damaged**. Neat patches, restitched straps, a rewrapped grip.
Damage would say he loses fights. Repair says he keeps going, which is the whole man. The
cloth on the sword is older than the rest of it and better cared for; he re-wraps that.

The greatbow is taller than he is and unstrung while he walks, so he leans on it: the
thing that makes him dangerous is also the thing holding him up. He carries what he gives
away, a satchel and a bottle, a traveller's load and not an adventurer's.

His face is forgettable on purpose, shaded under a hood or a wide hat, and there is
nothing underneath it — no scar, no eyepatch, no single striking feature. He introduces
himself every time because he assumes he has been forgotten, and that only lands if the
player genuinely cannot hold his face.

Refuse: armour that reads as a class, a billowing cloak, anything that glows, a companion,
and any feature memorable enough to function as a signature. He is not a reward.

Two animation notes. He never idle-fidgets; he stands the way a man stands who is about to
start walking again. And when he is knocked down, the getting up takes more frames than
the falling.

A recurring mortal character inspired by the archetype of Gael from Dark Souls.

He is not chosen.

He is not secretly the strongest person alive.

He is simply a weary traveler who **keeps walking**.

He appears at different points throughout the game.

Eventually he accompanies the player against Armageddon (§IX) — the wyvern, three times
the size of the small gold thing in the Font (§XIX) and worse than her. He comes to that
fight and to no other, and he does not say why.

He was there when that animal lost its eye, and he did nothing (§IX, *Pugream*). His
teacher got close enough to take it and did not come back from it; he watched, and he
lived, and he has been walking ever since. He has never mentioned it. Nothing in that
story makes him strong, and the encounter must not pretend otherwise.

He fights alongside the player without trivializing the encounter.

He gets knocked down.

He gets back up.

He keeps going.

His significance comes from persistence rather than power.

He represents something the gods have largely forgotten:

> **A mortal does not need to control the world to have meaning within it.**

### The Greatbow

**[LOCKED — the break, the handover, the materials, and that the rebuild is weaker. The
name and the text are PROPOSAL.]**

He breaks his great bow on Armageddon (§IX). When it is over he hands the player the
pieces. He does not make a speech about it and he does not stay to see what is done with
them.

**It cannot be put back.** The wood is not a wood anybody here works with and nobody the
player can ask has seen it before. The string is hair, from an animal that does not live
in this land. What the player ends up with is a rebuild: a real endgame weapon, and a
worse one than the thing they watched him draw an hour earlier.

That is §I delivered as a stat line rather than a sentence. **Nothing in its prime.** The
best bow in the world is a copy of a better bow, made out of what was left, by somebody
who did not know what they were handling — and the game showed the player the original,
working, once, in somebody else's hands, and then took it away for good.

**[PROPOSAL] Name: Greatbow of a Pilgrim.** Indefinite article. He is not named on it.

**[PROPOSAL] Flavour:**

> *'The string is hair. He did not say whose.'*

**[PROPOSAL] Lore verses:**

> Rebuilt from a break, and it does not draw as it did.
> The man who carried it spent it on one animal.
> The wood is not from here.
> Nobody asked him and he is still walking.

**[OPEN]** Where the materials are from, and whether they are his or his teacher's. The
book has one other person who came from outside (§XXVII) and it is not this one; do not
join them.

**Mechanics live in `PAD_Item_Plan.md`**, not here. One thing from it belongs in the
lore, because it is the lore: what the original had over the rebuild is **one second**.
His drew in five and this one takes six. Not more damage and not another effect — the same
weapon, in time. Everything the player watches him do in that fight is exactly what they
can do afterwards, and never quite quickly enough.

### His Place in the Book

**[LOCKED in principle; the details are in the planning document]**

Every faction in this world wants to use the player — the houses through the Guide, the demons through the doll, the Low Court through the orb, the Mechanic through her work. **He is the only person who wants nothing.** That is his entire function, and it only pays if he shows up early, shows up often, and never asks for anything, so that by the time the player has been everyone's instrument the man who never used them is the one they would follow.

He carries a bow. Everything else in this mod is an argument about reaching what you should not reach; he carries a tool. (The sword is not his. See the head of this section.)

**He introduces himself every time.** He assumes the player has forgotten. Against a god whose tragedy is that *none remember his name* (§XVI), the one mortal who keeps offering his is the counterweight the book needs.

His introduction, his gifts and which boss he fights beside are drafted in the planning document.

# PART VII — ENDINGS

*Where all of it is allowed to land.*

## §XXXII. Endings

The world can culminate in several philosophical outcomes.

### 1. Mortal / Defiance Ending

The player rejects the authority of the gods.

Mortals become independent.

The divine order begins to collapse.

Freedom is achieved, but the consequences are uncertain.

### 2. Loi / Order Ending

The player accepts Loi's authority.

The world becomes more stable and ordered.

But mortals surrender some degree of freedom and self-determination.

### 3. Mors / Death Ending

**[LOCKED]**

Mors's memories are restored. Death recognises the finality of death again.

What is restored is **memory, not mortality**. He was never going to die and still cannot (§VIII). He simply remembers how to make other things stop.

**Death itself does not disappear.** The world remembers how to die.

#### What It Costs

Everyone who had been living on the amnesia now goes, the Mechanic among them (§XXI). **The town does not empty out** — most of it survives — but the world is different in a way the player can feel rather than read:

- **The town stops fighting.** NPCs no longer help in boss battles. Nobody announces this. The player notices the Party Girl has stopped firing, and much later somebody says one thing about it.
- **The Nurse becomes the most important person in town and never mentions it.** Before, she patched up people who would have come back regardless. Now she is the only thing standing between anyone and an actual ending. Her rates do not change. Her dialogue does.

#### The Gift

**[LOCKED — the ending's sting]**

Mors thanks the player specifically: **the player alone remains respawnable.**

Everyone else gets to end. The player does not.

He means it kindly, and it is a sentence. The player is left as the last deathless thing in a world that has just learned how to die — unfinishable, unaccountable, exactly the abomination the Low Court was founded to hunt (§XVII). Death's gratitude creates the next god problem, and hands it to the person who fixed him.

The warm reading is available too, and is not exclusive: it may not be gratitude at all. He has only just got his memory back and does not trust it yet. Keeping one person deathless is a bookmark — *someone I can always find again.*

It also answers the shoreline graveyard's open grave, which takes the player's first death and no other. That hole was dug for them and never used.

### 4. Birth / Continuation Ending

Death is suppressed further.

Nothing truly ends.

Creation continues indefinitely.

Life continues indefinitely.

Eventually, existence itself becomes horrifyingly crowded with things that should have ended.

### 5. Moon Ending

The player accepts the Moon Lord.

The boundaries between:

- mortal
- god
- monster
- machine
- world

begin to dissolve.

Reality becomes something fundamentally alien.

### 6. Low Court / Silence Ending

The player destroys the gods.

No divine authority remains.

No miracles.

No divine intervention.

No gods to worship.

The heavens become silent.

### 7. A Small Life Ending

The player rejects the cosmic struggle.

They choose an ordinary life with someone they love.

The world remains imperfect.

The gods remain part of history.

The player simply chooses to live a finite life rather than become a cosmic ruler.
